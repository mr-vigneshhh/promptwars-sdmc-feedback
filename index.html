01 — The Challenge

A problem statement, and very little time.

The problem statement was revealed only shortly before the build period began. There was no runway — just enough time to read it, understand what it was really asking for, plan an approach, and start building. Every hour after that had to count: understanding the problem, designing a solution, building it, testing it, and getting it submitted before the window closed.

Hours, not days, to go from problem to product

02 — Building

ElectionFlow.

ElectionFlow was built as an interactive platform to make the election process easier to understand — the stages it moves through, the timelines involved, the procedures behind each step, and the information voters and first-time participants often struggle to find in one place.

The goal was simple: turn something that usually feels bureaucratic and scattered into something anyone could open, follow, and actually understand.

04 — Reaching the Top 10

From building under pressure to reaching the Top 10.

ElectionFlow moved from submission into the Top 10 and advanced to the presentation round in front of the judges.

Rank #1 in the AI evaluation is not the same as ranking first in the hackathon overall. The AI score reflects the technical evaluation layer; the final placements were decided afterward, at the presentation round. Both are real, and both matter — they're just not the same thing.

Then came the stage.

I presented ElectionFlow in front of the judges and explained the idea, the problem it solves, and how I built it under the time limit.

I didn't receive one of the three awards.

But this story is not about asking for a trophy.

It is about recognition.

05 — A Suggestion

TOP 10
INNOVATOR
A certificate.
A moment on stage.
A simple reminder: you made it this far.

Not every student needs a trophy. Sometimes, a certificate and a moment on stage — saying "you made it to the Top 10" — can be enough to make a student believe they should build again. It costs very little to give, and it can carry a student through the next hackathon, the next idea, the next attempt.

The trophy may belong to three people, but the journey involved more than three.

06 — Going Back

I went back for feedback.

I went back — not to question the result, but to understand where I could improve. I approached one of the judges afterward and asked for guidance, because I genuinely wanted to know what to work on before the next hackathon.

07 — What I Learned

Eight things this hackathon taught me.

Building under real pressure, on a fixed clock.
Turning a problem statement into a working idea, fast.
Testing quickly, without the luxury of extra time.
Presenting on stage, in front of judges.
Explaining technical work in plain language.
Handling a result that wasn't what I'd hoped for.
Asking for feedback instead of walking away.
Continuing to improve after the event ends.
